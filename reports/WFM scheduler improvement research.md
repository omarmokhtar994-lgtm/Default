# Shape the skeleton to absorb breaks

The engine's remaining coverage gap is not where most of the recent effort went. On the real workbooks, break placement on a fixed skeleton is already optimal or within one interval. Every day of AE_AR_B2B and NMG_SP is proven optimal ([NIGHT_15](../evidence/NIGHT_15_JOINT_SHIFT_BREAK_BUILD.md)). The losses come from two other places. The first is **Stage-1 skeletons that cover every interval before breaks but are not shaped to absorb them**: M2 at about 110 of 117, H3 at about 92 of 105, H1 at 120-145 of 168. The second is **run-to-run variance of 5-34 intervals on identical input**, which is as large as the gap itself. The best return therefore comes from three things in order. First, cheap changes that turn variance into gains and stop the engine choosing skeletons by the wrong score: best-of-N selection on after-break coverage, a funded DNBS phase, and LP-visible shortfall variables. Second, medium builds that make Stage 1 pay for the break damage it causes: Stage-2 feedback cuts, a hybrid implicit-break term, and deficit-driven ALNS. Branch-and-price comes last, as a research project. The literature supports this order. Iterating a break-aware shift phase with a break phase until no improvement beat the prior best method on all instances of the shift-and-break design benchmarks ([Akkermans et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10479-019-03487-6)). Logic-based Benders work identifies "include a relaxation of the subproblem in the master" as the single most effective device ([Hooker](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312096713_Logic-based_Benders_decomposition_for_planning_and_scheduling_a_computational_analysis)). The engine's own prototypes show that exact joint models are correct but cannot be solved inside a 15-60 minute, 2-4 core budget. Nearly all the academic evidence here was read from abstracts and search snippets, not full texts. The last section lists exactly which claims that affects.

## The gap lives in the skeleton and in the dice, not in break placement

Four measured facts define the problem.

**Stage 2 is not the bottleneck any more.** After S2-PAR, the change that stopped assumption literals from forcing every break solve onto one worker, the summed end-to-end score rose from 827 to 913 with no workbook worse ([NIGHT_14](../evidence/NIGHT_14_TEST_SUITES_AND_BENCHMARKS.md)). DNBS then showed that per-day aggregated break models are proven optimal on AE_AR_B2B and NMG_SP ([NIGHT_15](../evidence/NIGHT_15_JOINT_SHIFT_BREAK_BUILD.md)).

**On the planted cases, the loss is entirely in fitting breaks into skeletons that were not built for them.** The engine's best skeletons already cover 117/117, 105/105 and 168/168 before breaks. On the planted skeleton, the break model finds 117/117 with no hint, even on one worker. On the engine's own skeletons, 600 s on 4 workers plateaus at 110 (M2) and 95 (H3) ([NIGHT_14](../evidence/NIGHT_14_TEST_SUITES_AND_BENCHMARKS.md)).

**Stage 1 is selecting and scoring skeletons with a proxy that points the wrong way.** On E2, the productive-basis profile credits each associate with 0.711 FTE in every hour, as if break loss were spread evenly. Under that proxy, stacking 11 people on some days scores better than 10 every day. When the model was pinned to the planted optimum, it scored that optimum worse ([NIGHT_14](../evidence/NIGHT_14_TEST_SUITES_AND_BENCHMARKS.md)). The 900 s DNBS A/B shows the same failure at scale. On H1, a 34-interval swing happened with DNBS inactive, because a different skeleton won: before-break targets were 121 and 168, listed in the same order as the arms whose after-break results were 117 and 83 ([NIGHT_15](../evidence/NIGHT_15_JOINT_SHIFT_BREAK_BUILD.md)). On that reading, **the skeleton with perfect pre-break coverage produced the worse schedule**.

**The noise floor is wide.** Cricut Chat has landed at 167, 171, 178 and 182 on the same input. Earlier work measured up to 6 intervals of movement even at a fixed seed, because multi-worker CP-SAT under a wall-clock limit is not deterministic ([SOLVER_OPTIMIZATION_EXPERIMENTS](../evidence/SOLVER_OPTIMIZATION_EXPERIMENTS.md)).

This pattern matches a known failure mode. Kiermaier, Frey and Bard use a tours-then-breaks MIP decomposition like this engine's. They report that break and shift flexibility alone moves under-coverage by **up to 16.68%** ([Kiermaier et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10951-019-00635-5)). Avramidis et al. show that separating staffing from scheduling "can be highly suboptimal" ([Avramidis et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0377221709000332)). Commercial tools show the same pattern: NICE CXone, Verint, Genesys Engage and Calabrio all document a *separate* break re-optimization pass on top of generation ([CXone](https://help.nicecxone.com/content/workforcemanagement/optimizationrules.htm); [Genesys](https://all.docs.genesys.com/PEC-WFM/Current/Supervisor/SchdRBWzdInrDy); [Calabrio](https://help.calabrio.com/doc/Content/user-guides/schedules/about-optimization-steps.htm)). Vendors also do not solve the problem jointly, so the engine is not behind the industry on structure. Its remaining gains lie in how Stage 1 anticipates breaks and in how the pipeline handles variance.

The exact joint route was tested and stalled for a specific reason. The aggregated tour × pattern-count model is exact: fixed to the planted plan it scores 117, and fixed to the engine's candidate it scores 108. But CP-SAT cold found nothing in 300 s. Warm-started from 108 it stayed at 108, and HiGHS and SCIP did the same. Column generation's LP bound did not exclude 117 and was "highly degenerate". Diving produced 75 ([NIGHT_15](../evidence/NIGHT_15_JOINT_SHIFT_BREAK_BUILD.md)). A deficit surrogate spreads small misses, and a threshold objective gives the LP almost nothing to push against. The next section follows that thread into CP-SAT.

## CP-SAT's LP never sees the target-hit objective

In OR-Tools 9.15, a constraint with `OnlyEnforceIf` enters the LP relaxation **only when `linearization_level >= 2`** ([linear_relaxation.cc](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/v9.15/ortools/sat/linear_relaxation.cc#L1262)). The default is level 1 ([sat_parameters.proto](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/v9.15/ortools/sat/sat_parameters.proto#L1648)), and LNS sub-solves run at level 0 ([cp_model_search.cc](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/v9.15/ortools/sat/cp_model_search.cc#L796)). The engine models target hits as reified linear constraints, so the LP in default workers sees none of the rows that define its primary objective. The corpus telemetry fits that picture. Across 103 Stage-1 solves that ran to their slice limit, the **median relative gap was 88.93%, and the tightest was 14.4%** ([NIGHT_02](../evidence/NIGHT_02_GAP_LEVER_IS_DEAD.md)). The objective also uses a 400,000,000:1 tier structure (same source). Dominating weights of that size are exact in CP-SAT's integer arithmetic. They do, however, weaken LP guidance and make LNS accept only top-tier moves. That last point is an inference in the notes, not a measured fact ([Primer](https://github.com/d-krupke/cpsat-primer/blob/main/chapters/parameters.md)).

The obvious switch has already been tried and failed. Setting `linearization_level=2` globally on a Voice Stage-1 probe gave 235 against a 237 baseline, inside the noise ([SOLVER_OPTIMIZATION_EXPERIMENTS](../evidence/SOLVER_OPTIMIZATION_EXPERIMENTS.md)). The Primer warns that top-level parameters also reach LNS workers and can "slow them down to the point of being ineffective" ([Primer](https://github.com/d-krupke/cpsat-primer/blob/main/chapters/parameters.md)). The better fix is to change the model, not the parameter. Add a non-enforced shortfall `short[t] >= target[t] - cov[t]`, `short[t] >= 0`. Link it to the indicator with a plain linear row, `short[t] <= target[t]·(1 - hit[t])`. Give `short` a small secondary weight below the hit tier. These rows are LP-visible at level 1, so reduced-cost, RINS and the `default_lp` worker get a gradient without any global parameter change. If one worker should also see the enforced rows, add a custom `max_lp` entry through `extra_subsolvers` rather than setting the level globally ([proto](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/v9.15/ortools/sat/sat_parameters.proto#L715)). No published benchmark compares reified-indicator and deficit formulations in CP-SAT. The code fact is solid. The size of the effect on this model is unknown and must be measured.

Worker allocation is the second lever. At n ≤ 4 workers, 9.15 runs n−1 full workers and a single thread shared by all LNS, feasibility-jump and RINS tasks ([cp_model_search.cc](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/v9.15/ortools/sat/cp_model_search.cc#L1000)). The worker count clearly matters here: **300 s on 4 workers matched 1,800 s on 1 worker on AE_AR_B2B, roughly a 6x effective speedup**. Going to 5 or 8 workers on a 4-core host gained nothing ([WORKER_THRESHOLD_RESULT](../evidence/WORKER_THRESHOLD_RESULT.md)). What has not been tested is the *split*. Setting `num_full_subsolvers=1` or `2` at 4 workers gives LNS two or three threads instead of one. Adding bound-oriented subsolvers (`core`, `lb_tree_search`, `objective_*`) to `ignore_subsolvers` stops them taking threads under a quality-only goal. Both follow from the code, and neither has published rostering measurements ([cp_model_search.cc](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/v9.15/ortools/sat/cp_model_search.cc#L861)). The S2-PAR episode also argues for checking every solve site's effective worker count in the logs. A setting that silently forces a solve down to one worker cost 86 summed intervals once already ([NIGHT_14](../evidence/NIGHT_14_TEST_SUITES_AND_BENCHMARKS.md)). The same applies to hints. A hint that is complete and feasible becomes an incumbent at once. A partial one only steers a 10-conflict `HINT_SEARCH` ([cp_model_solver.cc](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/v9.15/ortools/sat/cp_model_solver.cc#L909)). Every warm-started solve should show `complete_hint` in its log.

## Variance is worth as many intervals as most algorithm changes

Performance variability is a structural property of tree search, not a defect. Tree search acts as "a chaotic amplifier" of tiny perturbations ([Fischetti & Monaci](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.2013.1231)), and MIPLIB treats performance as "a random variable that can be sampled" ([MIPLIB 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12532-011-0025-9)). Default parallel CP-SAT adds timing nondeterminism on top of that. Only `num_workers=1`, or `interleave_search=true` with `max_deterministic_time`, is reproducible ([proto](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/v9.15/ortools/sat/sat_parameters.proto#L771)).

Order statistics put a value on this spread. The expected maximum of k standard-normal draws is about 0.56σ for k=2, 1.03σ for k=4 and 1.42σ for k=8. Taking the engine's 5-34-interval spread as roughly 3σ over ten runs gives σ ≈ 1.6-11 intervals. **Best-of-4 on after-break score should then gain about 2-11 intervals over a typical single run, and it never returns the bad tail.** The Chat observations make this concrete. Their mean is 174.5 and their SD 6.8, so best-of-4 would have shipped 182, 7.5 intervals above the average run. That is more than DNBS gained on any fixed pool except H1. Restart and portfolio theory agrees whenever the outcome distribution is wide or heavy-tailed ([Gomes et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1006314320276); [Gomes & Selman](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0004370200000813)). Bet-and-run offers a structured variant. Friedrich et al. found that 40 short runs at 1% of the budget each, followed by continuing the best run, consistently beat a single run on TSP and vertex cover ([Friedrich et al.](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/2017/friedrich2017aaai-generic/)).

On 2-4 cores the catch is real. Splitting a 4-core host into several low-worker runs gives up the measured 6x speedup of the internal portfolio. Parallel best-of-N is therefore free only where production can use extra machines or containers. On one host, the choice between 1×4-worker and 2×2-worker runs has to be measured. A sequential split is plausible for easy workbooks: AE_AR_B2B and AE_FR_B2B reached 100% at 1,800 s on 4 workers, which suggests QUICK's 3,600 s is at least 2x more than they need ([NIGHT_03](../evidence/NIGHT_03_OOM_FIXED_AND_CEILING_AT_4_WORKERS.md)). No hard workbook has that curve measured. The cheapest version needs no extra compute at all. The engine already produces many skeletons through its 15-profile Stage-1 portfolio. It should **rank them by an after-break estimate** from a fast per-day aggregated break model, as DNBS builds, instead of by before-break coverage. Six of those 15 profiles have never executed, including one that reached a higher Voice before-target than the winning run ([NIGHT_01](../evidence/NIGHT_01_PROFILE_PORTFOLIO_FACTS.md)). The portfolio budget is therefore also worth re-examining.

The same variance undermines the evidence base. The 900 s DNBS A/B recorded a 34-interval swing between arms that behaved identically ([NIGHT_15](../evidence/NIGHT_15_JOINT_SHIFT_BREAK_BUILD.md)). With per-instance σ of 6 and two seeds per arm, the standard error of a per-instance difference is σ√(2/m) = 6 intervals. Ten seeds bring it to 2.7, and twenty to 1.9. Detecting a 3-interval mean effect at α=0.05 with 80% power, when the SD of per-instance differences is 6, needs about **31 instances** under n ≈ 7.85(σ_d/δ)². These are the notes' calculations from standard formulas, and the arithmetic checks out. The real corpus has about a dozen runnable workbooks. Planted synthetic cases from the existing generator therefore have to make up the instance count.

## Make Stage 1 pay for the breaks it causes

Four medium builds follow from the literature. The engine's own history says which forms of each will and will not work.

**Feedback cuts and iteration.** Akkermans, Post and Uetz build shifts in Phase 1 "while heuristically taking breaks into account", then assign breaks, and repeat until no improvement ([Akkermans et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10479-019-03487-6)). Restrepo, Gendron and Rousseau put daily break subproblems under a tour master, add integer Benders cuts, and outperform the prior branch-and-price ([Restrepo et al. 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305054818300145)). For this engine the loop is: skeleton → DNBS or Stage 2 per day → cuts back into Stage 1 → re-solve with a complete hint. Two kinds of cut are available. A window "Hall-type" cut requires on-floor agent-intervals in window W, minus the breaks that must fall inside W, to be at least the target. A per-day no-good says that if day d's start-count vector is unchanged, its loss is at least the measured value. The per-day structure is what makes this cheap. The subproblem is already fast and near-optimal. This is the design with the most direct mechanism against the M2/H3/H1 gaps, and none of its pieces has been tried.

**Implicit break variables, as a hybrid rather than a replacement.** The Bechtold-Jacobs, Aykin and Rekik line models breaks as aggregate counts linked by forward/backward constraints. It reaches the explicit model's LP relaxation with far fewer variables: under 1,500 against "hundreds of millions" in a continuous tour setting ([Brusco & Jacobs](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.46.12.1630.12074)). Rekik, Cordeau and Soumis extend it to multiple breaks with work-stretch (spacing) limits ([Rekik et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10951-009-0114-z)). The engine has tried this. The objective was right: it scored the planted plan 1.5e5 against 3.4e10 for the engine's skeleton. The search did not converge in 120-180 s on 4 workers ([NIGHT_14](../evidence/NIGHT_14_TEST_SUITES_AND_BENCHMARKS.md)). Better tuning is not the lesson. The implicit term should enter as Hooker's "relaxation in the master": a secondary term, or a lexicographic polish phase warm-started from the best current skeleton, with the existing objective kept as the primary driver. It can also serve as the after-break ranking function in the best-of-N selection described above.

**Deficit-driven ALNS.** LNS with an exact repair dominates recent large-instance rostering and driver scheduling. Legrain et al. embed rotation-based branch-and-price in LNS for up to 120 nurses over 8 weeks ([Legrain et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12532-019-00172-4)). Kletzander, Musliu et al. use column-generation repair and improve or prove optimal 48 of 50 best-knowns in bus-driver scheduling with complex breaks ([Kletzander et al.](https://www.jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/19027)). CP-SAT's generic neighbourhoods cannot see this structure, because a Boolean time-indexed model gets no scheduling neighbourhoods ([cp_model_lns.h](https://github.com/google/or-tools/blob/v9.15/ortools/sat/cp_model_lns.h#L681)). The engine's failed attempts mark the boundaries. One-day joint re-optimization gained 104→104 once the concurrency cap was respected. Two-day LNS on the exact model stayed at 108 after 16 sub-solves ([NIGHT_14](../evidence/NIGHT_14_TEST_SUITES_AND_BENCHMARKS.md); [NIGHT_15](../evidence/NIGHT_15_JOINT_SHIFT_BREAK_BUILD.md)). The missing moves are the ones that restructure the skeleton: day-swap operators that change days-off patterns and move capacity between days, break-congestion operators that stagger starts whose break windows collide, and deficit-window operators seeded from post-break shortfalls. Each candidate should be judged by DNBS plus `calculate_metrics`, the acceptance oracle that already caught two false gains ([NIGHT_15](../evidence/NIGHT_15_JOINT_SHIFT_BREAK_BUILD.md)).

**Tour-pool recombination.** Weekly tours from all LNS incumbents and portfolio candidates can be pooled and re-solved as a set-partitioning CP-SAT model. This captures part of what price-and-branch offers without writing a pricer. The engine's SCIP run over 1,190 column-generation tours reached only 42 of 117, which shows that the pool's makeup decides everything ([NIGHT_15](../evidence/NIGHT_15_JOINT_SHIFT_BREAK_BUILD.md)). Pools drawn from real incumbents are the untested variant.

## Branch-and-price is the ceiling, not the next step

Personalized branch-and-price with grammar-based pricing reaches **gaps under 1%** on one-week instances with up to 5 activities ([Restrepo et al. 2016](https://www.cirrelt.ca/documentstravail/cirrelt-2015-03.pdf)). At scale, exact methods stall: stabilized branch-and-price for tour plus meal-break scheduling reports gaps **up to 7% at 65 workers** under a 1,000-node limit ([Brunner & Stolletz](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/35187/)). Two things set this engine apart from those papers. The engine's primary objective counts threshold hits, which no retrieved paper studies. Its own column-generation prototype showed that the LP is degenerate under that objective ([NIGHT_15](../evidence/NIGHT_15_JOINT_SHIFT_BREAK_BUILD.md)). A real branch-and-price would need a per-associate weekly DP pricer (days-off pair, at most 2 shift types, 12 h rest), a piecewise-linear surrogate so the duals mean something, threshold-aware branching, and an LNS wrapper in the Legrain style to stay inside 60 minutes. That is months of work with an uncertain payoff. Its near-term value is as a source of bounds that show how much gap is actually left.

## Ranked recommendations

The ranking weighs expected coverage gain against effort and risk on this engine's measured behaviour. Every item ships only on a pre-registered A/B. The protocol has seven parts:

1. **Primary metric.** BEST_FINAL after_target, declared before any run.
2. **Seeds.** At least 10 per arm on the hard cases: Chat, Voice, NMG_SP, M2, H3 and H1, plus new planted instances to reach about 30.
3. **Configuration.** Production settings: 2-4 workers, wall-clock budget, dedicated cores.
4. **Test.** Wilcoxon signed-rank on per-instance mean differences, with a bootstrap CI.
5. **Tail check.** Worst-of-m reported alongside the mean.
6. **Validity gate.** A check that the new phase actually executed, as DNBS rule (5) did.
7. **Replication.** A held-out confirmation set.

Where a change can be isolated, noise-free paired tests on saved candidate pools, or deterministic `interleave_search` runs, should come before end-to-end runs. Racing (F-Race) can stop clear losers early without inflating false positives ([Birattari et al.](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-02538-9_13); [Campelo & Takahashi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.02997)).

## What the notes could not verify

Egress blocked the full text of almost every academic, vendor and preprint source. Only GitHub-hosted material was read directly: the OR-Tools v9.15 source, `troubleshooting.md` and the CP-SAT Primer. The CP-SAT code facts above (enforced constraints and LP level, the thread split, LNS internals, hint handling) are therefore verified. The literature claims are not.

The following literature claims rest on abstracts or search snippets only:

- **Akkermans et al.** How breaks are "heuristically taken into account", and by how much the method wins, could not be read.
- **Restrepo et al. 2018.** The report has only "outperforms" the earlier branch-and-price, with no numbers.
- **Abstract-level figures.** Kiermaier's 16.68% and Brunner & Stolletz's 7% come from abstracts.
- **Kletzander et al.** The 48/50 result and the claim of a gap under 1% on more than half the instances are abstract-level.
- **Implicit-model exactness.** Rekik's exactness under extraordinary overlap and with multiple breaks is unverified, as is the equivalence of the Aykin and Bechtold-Jacobs LP relaxations. The latter is a secondary claim.
- **Legrain et al.** The instance sizes are unverified.
- **Friedrich et al.** The 40×1% configuration is from a snippet. Fischetti & Monaci's speedups, which are not reported here, could not be read.
- **Multi-worker symmetry.** The claim that symmetry constraints hurt multi-worker CP-SAT (arXiv 2412.10272) is snippet-only.
- **ReXi.** The primal-integral figures come from a non-peer-reviewed 2026 preprint, read as a snippet.
- **Background knowledge, not checked.** Ropke & Pisinger's ALNS weights, Widl & Musliu's NP-completeness result, the INRC-II runner-up teams, and the claim that a linear shortfall term speeds CP-SAT on threshold objectives are all flagged as unverified background.
- **Vendor material.** Every vendor finding came from search extracts of help pages, and patent assignees (US7725339, US6823315) are unconfirmed.

Two internal conflicts also remain open. OR-Tools' troubleshooting doc says 8 workers are the "minimum" for parallel search, yet the code and the Primer run LNS from 2 workers. The engine's own measurement found no first-solution subsolver at 4 workers and one at 5 ([WORKER_THRESHOLD_RESULT](../evidence/WORKER_THRESHOLD_RESULT.md)). Finally, no published source measures CP-SAT seed variance on rostering, the throughput cost of `interleave_search`, or reified-versus-deficit formulations. The expected-impact figures in the table are therefore the engine's own measurements plus order-statistics arithmetic, not literature benchmarks.

## Conclusion

The engine's history reads as a sequence of hidden single-worker bottlenecks and proxy objectives, not missing algorithms. S2-PAR's +86 came from finding one. The E2 fix came from noticing that the productive-basis proxy punished the optimal plan. The H1 swing shows the same proxy problem at skeleton selection. So the highest-leverage question in the next round is not "which advanced method?". It is "which score is Stage 1 optimizing and selecting on, and does it know about breaks?". Everything in the quick-win tier answers that question cheaply, and several of these answers also make the medium builds easier. The after-break skeleton ranker becomes the evaluator for feedback cuts and for ALNS.

The second shift in thinking is about evidence. At the engine's noise level, one- or two-seed A/Bs are coin flips. The team has already seen that twice, with S2-PAR held back on a component test and DNBS "failing" while inactive. A 30-instance, 10-seed protocol costs real machine time. But without it, a 5-interval gain and a 5-interval loss look the same, and the medium builds, where the remaining 8-30 intervals are, cannot be judged at all.
